PhD Researcher · Quantum Cybersecurity

Zeeshan Ajmal

I'm a doctoral researcher in computer science at the University of Oulu, in the Oulu University Secure Programming Group (OUSPG). I study how quantum computers can be attacked, and how AI and quantum machine learning can catch those attacks.

Quantum programs pass through compilers, cloud platforms and shared hardware before they run. Each step is a place where someone can tamper with a circuit. My work builds datasets and models that spot that tampering, so quantum software can be trusted as it moves into real use.

The core question

Which gate doesn't belong?

A quantum program is compiled into a circuit of gates before it runs. An attacker in the toolchain or the cloud can slip in one extra gate that quietly changes the result. I train classical and quantum ML models to read a circuit and flag that kind of change.

What I work on

Four questions that run through my PhD.

Detecting malicious quantum circuits

I built a dataset of about 9,000 clean and tampered circuits in Qiskit and use it to train models that tell them apart.

QiskitQSVMRandom Forest

Attacks on quantum ML models

Testing how adversarial inputs, such as FGSM perturbations, fool variational quantum classifiers, and whether results from simulators hold on real hardware.

VQCFGSMSim-to-hardware

Shared quantum computers

When several users share one machine, crosstalk between qubits and changes to submitted circuits both open attack paths. I study both.

CrosstalkCircuit tampering

AI agents for quantum security

Exploring how agentic AI can monitor quantum workloads, explain what it finds and suggest a response.

Agentic AILLMsThreat detection

Background

How I got here

I started as an electrical and telecom engineer. My early work was in the field, setting up 3G and 4G radio sites and microwave links, and supporting a telecom regulator with ISO 27001 and compliance work.

I then spent several years in risk and security engineering for large industrial and data center sites. That work taught me root cause analysis, incident investigation and how to protect systems that cannot go down.

In 2023 I moved to Finland for a master's in computer science at the University of Oulu. My thesis looked at using AI to find malicious quantum circuits, and that question became my PhD, which I started in 2025. Alongside research, I work part time as Cyber Exposure & Vulnerability Lead at Arctic Security, which keeps my work tied to real security practice.
